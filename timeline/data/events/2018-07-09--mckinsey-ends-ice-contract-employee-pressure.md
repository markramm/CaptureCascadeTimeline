McKinsey & Company stops working for Immigration and Customs Enforcement following intense pressure from outraged employees and alumni. Kevin Sneader, the firm's new global managing partner, conveys the decision in a note to former employees stating the ICE contract 'rightly raised concerns.' In a letter to all staff, Sneader commits that McKinsey 'will not, under any circumstances, engage in any work, anywhere in the world, that advances or assists policies that are at odds with our values.'

Sneader's statement represents a rare victory for employee activism at a major consulting firm. Employees at McKinsey successfully force their leadership to terminate a lucrative government contract on moral grounds—a remarkable outcome in an industry known for working with controversial clients. The employee revolt demonstrates growing willingness among younger workers to challenge corporate ethics, even at elite firms where questioning client work traditionally risked career damage.

However, Sneader's assurance contains a critical caveat. He claims McKinsey's support 'has never been focused on developing, advising or implementing immigration policies, including the child-separation policy.' This statement would prove misleading when ProPublica later obtained documents showing McKinsey directly advised on detention operations and deportation acceleration—the core mechanisms implementing Trump's immigration policies.

Federal records confirm McKinsey's ICE work ends in July 2018. But the firm's commitment to avoiding controversial government work proves short-lived. One week after Sneader announces the end of ICE work, McKinsey signs a $2 million contract with Customs and Border Protection (CBP), the sister agency to ICE, demonstrating the firm's continued involvement in immigration enforcement through a different agency contract.
