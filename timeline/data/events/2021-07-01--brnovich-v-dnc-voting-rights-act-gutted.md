Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that Arizona's restrictions on out-of-precinct voting and third-party ballot collection do not violate Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, making it substantially harder to challenge discriminatory voting laws nationwide. Justice Alito authored the majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett, all Federalist Society affiliates. The decision established new "guideposts" for evaluating Section 2 challenges, requiring courts to consider the size of burdens, deviations from past practices, racial imbalances, and overall opportunity to vote. These heightened standards effectively raised the bar for proving voting discrimination, reversing decades of civil rights protections. Justice Kagan's dissent, joined by Breyer and Sotomayor, condemned the majority for remaking Section 2: "This Court has no right to remake Section 2... But Congress gets to make that call." The ruling came eight years after Shelby County v. Holder (2013) gutted Section 5's preclearance requirement. Bloomberg Law analysis found Section 2 citations in voting rights complaints declined nearly 60 percent in the 40 months following Brnovich compared to 54 months before, demonstrating the decision's chilling effect on civil rights enforcement. The decision advanced systematic voter suppression by state legislatures following the 2020 election, greenlighting restrictions on mail voting, ballot drop boxes, and voter assistance that disproportionately burden minority communities. Combined with the Roberts Court's campaign finance deregulation, the ruling consolidated plutocratic control by making it harder for marginalized voters to participate while unlimited money floods elections.
