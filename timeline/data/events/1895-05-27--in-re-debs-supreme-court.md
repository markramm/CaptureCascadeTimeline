The Supreme Court unanimously upheld Eugene Debs's imprisonment for violating an injunction during the Pullman Strike, establishing labor injunctions as a primary corporate weapon against unions. Justice David Brewer's opinion held that federal courts could issue injunctions based on the government's "power to regulate interstate commerce" and "property interest in the mail," dramatically expanding judicial reach beyond traditional civil matters into "preservation of national sovereignty and social order." Most significantly, the Court upheld using the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890—legislation designed to break up corporate monopolies—against a labor union, despite Congress never intending this application. The decision meant "the government successfully employed the Sherman Antitrust Act to prosecute the striking workers, setting a precedent that stood as an impediment to collective bargaining until the passage of the Clayton Antitrust Act in 1914." This judicial capture enabled corporations to use monopoly protections while criminalizing worker organizing, illustrating how the legal system was weaponized to protect capital against labor.
