Aaron Director becomes head of a new Volker Fund-financed study called the Antitrust Project (1953-1957) at University of Chicago Law School, systematically working to restructure American antitrust law to favor corporate interests over competition enforcement. Director co-chairs the project with Edward Levi, teaching antitrust courses that students compare to "religious conversion" while developing legal theories arguing that private monopolies only emerge from government action and would cease to be problems without regulatory intervention. The Antitrust Project continues the Volker Fund's financing of Chicago law and economics after the Free Market Study ended in 1952, demonstrating sustained corporate funding for reshaping legal doctrine. Director's enormous impact on antitrust law comes through the Antitrust Project's activities, his subsequent work at Chicago Law School, and his recruitment of scholars including Robert Bork, Richard Posner, Antonin Scalia, and Chief Justice William Rehnquist, whom he converts to Chicago School neoliberal doctrine favoring corporate consolidation over antitrust enforcement. Director's legal theories challenge the status quo antitrust positions of government officials and economists that had constrained corporate power, providing intellectual justification for reduced antitrust enforcement. This 1953 project precedes the Powell Memo by 18 years but implements Powell's later strategy of using universities to reshape legal doctrine, training future judges and policymakers to view monopoly power as natural rather than dangerous. The Antitrust Project represents systematic academic capture funded by corporate interests to undermine laws protecting competition, establishing the Chicago School approach that would dominate federal courts and enable massive corporate consolidation.
