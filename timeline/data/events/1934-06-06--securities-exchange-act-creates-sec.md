President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Securities Exchange Act into law, creating the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to regulate stock exchanges and prevent the abuses exposed by the Pecora Commission. FDR signed the legislation with Ferdinand Pecora, Benjamin Cohen, Thomas Corcoran, and James Landis standing by—the team that had drafted the law based on evidence compiled during the Senate Banking Committee investigation. The Act prohibited manipulation practices including fictitious trades and stock pools, established registration requirements for securities exchanges, and gave the SEC broad authority to define and prohibit manipulative devices. Section 16, known as "the anti-Wiggin provision," specifically prohibited corporate directors from short-selling their own company's stock, directly responding to Albert Wiggin's $4 million profit betting against Chase Bank during the 1929 crash. On July 2, 1934, Roosevelt appointed the first five-member bipartisan SEC commission including Ferdinand Pecora. Ironically, Roosevelt passed over Pecora for chairman, instead appointing Joseph P. Kennedy, who had participated in pool syndicate operations the year before. At the signing, Roosevelt asked Pecora: "Ferd, now that I have signed this bill and it has become law, what kind of law will it be?" Pecora replied: "It will be a good or bad bill, Mr. President, depending upon the men who administer it." This prescient observation would prove tragically accurate as regulatory capture and deregulation systematically weakened the SEC over subsequent decades.
