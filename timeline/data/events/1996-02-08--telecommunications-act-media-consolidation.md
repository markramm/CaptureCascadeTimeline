President Bill Clinton signs the Telecommunications Act of 1996 on February 8, 1996, at the Library of Congress, using an electronic pen in the first U.S. law to be signed electronically. The legislation represents the first major overhaul of telecommunications law in 62 years and passes despite being "one of the most heavily lobbied pieces of legislation Congress had yet seen." The Act significantly reduces regulations on media concentration and cross-ownership, enabling companies like AOL/Time Warner, Viacom, and Clear Channel to purchase multiple media outlets in local markets. The number of major media content companies shrinks from 50 in 1983 to 10 in 1996, and just 6 by 2005, with these six corporations eventually controlling 90% of the country's major media. Radio consolidation proves particularly severe: an FCC study finds drastic decline in the number of station owners even as total stations increase, resulting in radio homogenization where local programming and content disappears. The lobbying effort involves 350 industry lobbyists from companies including BellSouth and Southwestern Bell literally drafting deregulation bills through Tom DeLay's secretive Project Relief group, coordinating campaign contributions for supporters. The Act demonstrates how corporate regulatory capture can fundamentally reshape democratic infrastructure by concentrating information control, eliminating local journalism and diverse viewpoints, and creating media monopolies that will enable future propaganda operations including Sinclair Broadcasting's coordinated messaging campaigns.
