Jamaica enters its second stand-by arrangement with the IMF under Prime Minister Michael Manley's democratic socialist government, initiating what becomes decades of devastating structural adjustment programs. Manley, elected in 1972 on a platform of reducing dependency on foreign capital and fighting inequality, had pursued progressive reforms including nationalizing key industries, increasing revenue from bauxite extraction, improving workers' rights, and expanding educational opportunities. However, the 1970s energy crisis forces his government to take loans for fuel-based imports, and with the economy in recession, unemployment rising, and debt growing rapidly, Manley reluctantly turns to the IMF. The Fund's harsh proposed reforms, including laying off 10,000 public sector workers, prove so unpalatable that Manley ends ties with the IMF just before the 1980 election. The IMF explicitly works to ensure Manley's defeat, with structural adjustment programs designed to reverse his reform agenda. Opposition leader Edward Seaga wins in 1980 and immediately re-engages with the IMF, becoming a model case for structural adjustment in the 1980s. Under IMF pressure, Seaga lays off 30,000 public sector workers; registered nurses fall by 60%; food subsidies are abolished while the IMF holds down wages; and health, education, and housing systems collapse. When interest rates rise in the early 1980s, Jamaica's debt payments surge from 16% of exports in 1977 to 35% by 1986, giving international institutions crushing leverage. Jamaica—which had no national debt before 1970—accumulates $4 billion in debt through the 1970s-1980s. By the time Manley returns to office in the 1990s, he accepts neoliberal policies as inevitable. Jamaica ultimately repays more money ($19.8 billion) than borrowed ($18.5 billion) yet still owes $7.8 billion due to compound interest, spending twice as much on debt service as on education and health combined, demonstrating the structural violence of debt-trap economics.
