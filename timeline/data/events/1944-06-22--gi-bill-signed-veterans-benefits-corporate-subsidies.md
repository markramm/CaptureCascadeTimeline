President Franklin D. Roosevelt signs the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, commonly known as the G.I. Bill of Rights, on June 22, 1944. The comprehensive legislation provides education, housing, and business benefits to returning WWII veterans while simultaneously subsidizing corporate training programs and driving expansion of universities and the housing industry. Through the Veterans Administration, the act enabled veterans to obtain grants for school and college tuition, subsistence, books, supplies, and equipment; low-interest mortgage loans for homes; low-interest loans for farms or businesses; job training and hiring privileges; one year of unemployment compensation; and counseling services. Within seven years, approximately 8 million veterans received educational benefits: 2.3 million attended colleges and universities, 3.5 million received school training, and 3.4 million received on-the-job training. Professor Edwin Amenta notes that veterans benefits were a bargain for conservatives who feared high taxation and extension of New Deal agencies, as benefits went to a small group without long-term implications for universal programs, diverting power from New Deal bureaucracies. The GI Bill provided massive subsidies to universities and colleges (driving unprecedented expansion), the housing industry (low-interest mortgage loans fueled suburban development), corporations (on-the-job training meant government paid companies to train workers), and the banking industry (VA-guaranteed loans eliminated risk while ensuring profits). The benefits were not distributed equally - discriminatory lending and admission practices meant Black veterans often could not access the same opportunities as white veterans, contributing to the racial wealth gap.
