The National Association of Manufacturers launches Industry on Parade, a weekly television series promoting capitalist industrial production distributed free to television stations nationwide, pioneering systematic corporate propaganda through mass media. Each 13.5-minute episode highlights American manufacturing and business while including anticommunist messages and "public service announcements" promoting capitalism, the American way, and rewards of a free economy. NAM collaborates with NBC from 1950-1953, then producer Arthur Lodge forms his own company continuing production until 1960, ultimately creating over 500 episodes broadcast on 270 stations nationally and foreign stations in 33 countries by 1957. The series promotes corporate power by documenting hundreds of companies and products, featuring stories arranged thematically around specific industries or products, each episode prominently including anticommunist messaging. The program wins a Peabody Award for National Public Service in 1954 despite being corporate propaganda, demonstrating successful corporate capture of public discourse through media. The series's success provokes the AFL-CIO to demand equivalent television time, which they use to broadcast Americans at Work. This 1950s media capture strategy precedes the Powell Memo's 1971 call for business to reach "the public generally" through television and other media by over 20 years, proving that corporate America already understood and deployed systematic media strategies for shaping public opinion before Powell articulated the blueprint. NAM's Industry on Parade represents early infrastructure for the corporate media ecosystem Powell later urged business to build.
