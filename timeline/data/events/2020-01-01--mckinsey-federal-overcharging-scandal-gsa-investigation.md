The General Services Administration (GSA) terminates McKinsey's federal multiple award schedule contract after the GSA Inspector General determines the firm cheated the government out of $65 million by charging prices 10% higher than initially proposed. This scandal exposes systematic overcharging of federal agencies and raises questions about why the U.S. government continues to pay premium prices for consulting services that could be provided by direct government employees at a fraction of the cost.

By 2019, McKinsey works for more than 15 federal agencies and departments, and 25 states, despite being caught overcharging. Critics point out that the government spends far too much on management consulting: in its 2024 budget, the Biden administration requests $70 billion for 'professional services' (management consulting), which represents 5% of all discretionary spending.

The scandal reveals how consulting firm pricing works: the Industrial Funding Fee structure allows agencies to pay $3 million per year for a 23-year-old McKinsey employee instead of hiring an experienced person directly to do IT management. The government agency in charge of bulk buying (GSA) isn't incentivized to save money but rather to facilitate spending, creating a system where overpriced consulting contracts are the norm rather than the exception.

McKinsey's federal work is negatively impacted by the contract termination, but the firm continues to receive substantial state and local government contracts. The overcharging scandal is part of a broader pattern where consulting firms extract excessive fees from government clients who face no market discipline and where the consultants who approve contracts often move to consulting firms themselves, creating revolving door conflicts of interest. The $65 million in overcharges could have funded significant public services or direct government hiring instead of enriching McKinsey partners.
