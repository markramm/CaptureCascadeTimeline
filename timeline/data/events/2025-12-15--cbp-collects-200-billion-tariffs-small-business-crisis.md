U.S. Customs and Border Protection announced it had collected over $200 billion in tariffs between January 20 and December 15, 2025, through more than 40 Trump executive orders. The agency celebrated the "record-breaking" collections as a policy success.

Meanwhile, a CNBC investigation revealed small businesses are turning to predatory merchant cash advances with interest rates exceeding 30% to cover tariff costs. These unregulated loans—not subject to FDIC oversight—have pushed businesses into financial crisis. Center for American Progress analysis found small-business importers paid an average of $151,000 in additional tariffs from April-September 2025.

The $200 billion in tariffs represents a massive tax on American importers and consumers. While framed as targeting foreign countries, tariffs are paid by U.S. companies that import goods, who then pass costs to consumers through higher prices or absorb them through reduced profits and layoffs.

The combination of record tariff collections and small business distress illustrates the policy's regressive impact: large corporations can absorb tariff costs or restructure supply chains, while small businesses lacking capital reserves resort to predatory lending that accelerates their financial deterioration.
