Canadian author and social activist Naomi Klein published 'The Shock Doctrine: The Rise of Disaster Capitalism,' a comprehensive investigation exposing how neoliberal economic policies promoted by Milton Friedman and the Chicago School of economics rose to global prominence through a deliberate strategy of exploiting crises. The book examines how political actors exploit the chaos of natural disasters, wars, and economic crises to push through unpopular policies like deregulation, privatization, and austerity that would face massive resistance under normal circumstances. Klein identifies Russia's post-Soviet shock therapy as a quintessential case study, spanning the investigation across four decades from Chile after Pinochet's coup to Russia after the Soviet collapse, from Baghdad under U.S. 'Shock and Awe' to New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina. The book details how Western financial powers swooped into Russia during the financial crisis of the early 1990s and refused to provide aid until Russia agreed to radical reworking of its economy, implemented as shock therapy with many draconian changes instituted simultaneously with little warning. Klein argues these changes, which devastated the lower classes, required severe political repression to enforce the transition and resulted in the deaths of millions of Russians. The book shows how the University of Chicago under Milton Friedman produced leading neo-conservative and neo-liberal thinkers whose influence remained profound in Washington. Klein describes the technique as using crisis-induced shock and extreme violence to implement economic policies that create free markets even less regulated than those existing before the Great Depression. 'The Shock Doctrine' became an international bestseller translated into over 30 languages, fundamentally reshaping public understanding of neoliberal economics and providing a theoretical framework that connected Russia's oligarch creation, Chile's Pinochet dictatorship, Iraq's occupation, and numerous other cases of what Klein termed 'disaster capitalism.' The book's analysis of Russia specifically validated critics who argued that U.S.-backed reforms intentionally created oligarchic kleptocracy rather than democracy.
