The Financial Oversight and Management Board (FOMB) retained McKinsey & Company in late November 2016 as its lead strategic consultant, initiating what would become one of the most egregious conflicts of interest in bankruptcy history. McKinsey was awarded contracts worth approximately $945,000 monthly (rising to $1.4 million monthly by 2017, then $2 million by 2018) despite its investment subsidiary MIO Partners owning at least $20 million in Puerto Rico bonds—making McKinsey simultaneously an adviser to the board and a creditor with financial interests in the bankruptcy outcome. When applying for the advisory role, McKinsey told the board it knew of no conflicts 'or potential appearance of a conflict of interest' and failed to disclose MIO's Puerto Rico bond holdings. The PROMESA law uniquely exempted board advisers from the stringent conflict disclosure requirements imposed on all other bankruptcy professionals, enabling McKinsey to avoid filing the thousands of pages of conflict documentation required of banks, law firms, and other consultants. Emails obtained by Centro de Periodismo Investigativo revealed McKinsey's central role in board operations: establishing frameworks for budget analysis, organizing decision-making processes, developing fiscal plans, and advising on virtually every aspect of the board's work. The selection committee—Carlos M. García, Ana J. Matosantos, and José R. González—approved McKinsey's appointment in October 2016 through an RFP process that failed to uncover or address the firm's bond holdings. By January 2019, McKinsey had collected over $72 million from the board, with ongoing contracts totaling $3.3 million monthly—all while its subsidiaries profited from restructuring decisions it helped craft.
