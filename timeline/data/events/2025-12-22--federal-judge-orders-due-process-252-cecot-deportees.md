A federal judge orders the U.S. government to provide due process to 252 Venezuelan men sent to El Salvador's CECOT mega-prison, giving the administration until January 5, 2026 to either return them to the U.S. or provide hearings. The ruling challenges Trump's invocation of the 1798 Alien Enemies Act—only the fourth time in U.S. history the law has been used—under the claim that the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua is part of the Venezuelan government.

The U.S. is paying El Salvador $6 million to house migrants at CECOT, a facility documented by Human Rights Watch for "arbitrary arrests, enforced disappearances, torture and other ill-treatment." CBS News investigations found most of those sent had no apparent criminal records. The 280 people transferred in March-April 2025 were held incommunicado for four months without any outside contact.

The case of Kilmar Ábrego Garcia, wrongly deported despite legal protection from persecution, was released this month after a separate federal court order—demonstrating that some sent to CECOT had legitimate legal claims to remain in the U.S.

The use of a 226-year-old wartime law to circumvent due process protections represents a constitutional crisis. By sending detainees to a foreign torture facility, the administration attempts to place them beyond the reach of U.S. courts—an offshore detention strategy reminiscent of Guantanamo Bay but applied to civil immigration cases rather than terrorism suspects.
