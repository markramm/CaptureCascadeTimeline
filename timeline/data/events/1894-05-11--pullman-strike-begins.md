Pullman factory workers struck after 25 percent wage cuts during the 1893 depression, escalating to a nationwide railway boycott by 125,000 American Railway Union members by June 26th under Eugene V. Debs. President Cleveland deployed federal troops on July 3-4, 1894, ostensibly to ensure mail delivery but actually to break the strike through a federal court injunction. The violence killed 34 people nationwide, including 13 in Chicago alone, with 53 seriously wounded. Federal or state troops entered Nebraska, Iowa, California, Colorado, Oklahoma and Illinois. Debs was arrested July 11th for violating the injunction. The Supreme Court's unanimous In re Debs decision (1895) upheld using the Sherman Antitrust Act—designed to break up corporate monopolies—against unions, establishing injunctions as a primary strikebreaking tool. This precedent "stood as an impediment to collective bargaining until the passage of the Clayton Antitrust Act in 1914," demonstrating how corporate interests captured judicial power to criminalize labor organizing while protecting corporate monopolies.
