English economist John Williamson, senior fellow at the Institute for International Economics, coins the term 'Washington Consensus' and outlines ten specific policy reforms representing agreement among Washington-based institutions (U.S. Treasury, IMF, World Bank, think tanks) on measures to restore macroeconomic stability in Latin American countries amid the debt crisis. The ten prescriptions include: fiscal discipline, redirecting public expenditure, tax reform, financial liberalization, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, eliminating barriers to foreign direct investment, privatization of state-owned enterprises, deregulation, and secure property rights. Though Williamson claims to describe existing consensus rather than prescribe market fundamentalism, the framework becomes the intellectual foundation for structural adjustment programs (SAPs) imposed by international financial institutions on indebted developing countries throughout the 1990s and 2000s. The debt crisis of the 1970s-1980s gives the Bretton Woods institutions unprecedented leverage to force countries to accept these reforms as prerequisites for financial assistance. Despite claims of promoting growth, structural adjustment programs consistently produce mixed results at best: while achieving macroeconomic stability in some cases, they generally fail to deliver economic growth and instead create vicious cycles of low investment, reduced social spending, reduced consumption, and stagnant output. The Washington Consensus becomes synonymous with neoliberal shock therapy despite over 100 countries under adjustment showing few success stories, establishing the ideological framework that enables massive wealth extraction from Global South countries to international creditors and multinational corporations.
