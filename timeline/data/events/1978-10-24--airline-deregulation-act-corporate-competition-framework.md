President Jimmy Carter signs the Airline Deregulation Act into law, eliminating federal control over airline routes, schedules, and fares and establishing deregulation as bipartisan political consensus despite strong airline industry opposition. Carter, appointing Alfred Kahn, a Cornell economics professor, to chair the Civil Aeronautics Board in 1977, embraces Chicago School economic theory advocating free market competition over government regulation. While supported by economists, think tanks, and consumer advocates promising lower fares and increased competition, the legislation enables massive airline industry consolidation over subsequent decades, with dozens of carriers merging or failing and power concentrating in a handful of dominant airlines. The timing - just months after Bork's 1978 "Antitrust Paradox" publication and concurrent with Heritage Foundation expansion and conservative infrastructure building - demonstrates how deregulation ideology spans partisan lines, advanced through academic economics (Chicago School), think tank advocacy (Heritage, Cato supporting market fundamentalism), and bipartisan political consensus. The Act establishes the template for subsequent deregulation of trucking (Motor Carrier Act 1980), telecommunications, finance, and energy sectors, fundamentally reshaping government-business relationships in alignment with corporate preferences for reduced oversight. This represents ideological capture transcending party - Democratic Carter implementing Chicago School theories that will accelerate under Republican Reagan, demonstrating the effectiveness of academic and think tank infrastructure in reshaping policy consensus.
