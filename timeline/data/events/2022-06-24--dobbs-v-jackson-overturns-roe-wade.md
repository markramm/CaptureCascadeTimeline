Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion, overruling Roe v. Wade (1973) and Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992) after nearly 50 years, representing the culmination of a decades-long Federalist Society project. Justice Alito authored the majority opinion, joined by Justices Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Chief Justice Roberts concurred in judgment but did not support fully overturning Roe. The decision upheld Mississippi's Gestational Age Act banning most abortions after 15 weeks. Alito's reasoning asserted abortion is not "deeply rooted in the Nation's history and traditions," claiming that when the Fourteenth Amendment was adopted, three-quarters of states criminalized abortion. The ruling immediately triggered abortion bans in 13 states with "trigger laws" and enabled restrictive legislation across Republican-controlled states. All five justices in the majority were appointed with direct assistance from Leonard Leo and the Federalist Society, which spent decades building the judicial infrastructure to overturn Roe. Justices Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett were nominated by Trump from lists prepared by Leo and the Federalist Society. During confirmation hearings, Kavanaugh called Roe "settled law" and "precedent on precedent," while Barrett avoided stating her views, raising questions about whether they misled senators. The dissent by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan warned that overturning Roe threatens other privacy rights including contraception, same-sex marriage, and intimate relations. Justice Thomas's concurrence explicitly called for reconsidering substantive due process precedents protecting these rights. The decision marked the most significant reversal of individual rights by the Supreme Court in modern history, enabling state control over reproductive autonomy and demonstrating the success of right-wing legal movement's institutional capture strategy.
