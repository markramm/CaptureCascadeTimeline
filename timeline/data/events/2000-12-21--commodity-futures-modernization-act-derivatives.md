President Bill Clinton signs the Commodity Futures Modernization Act (CFMA) into law on December 21, 2000, after Congress passes it on December 15. The legislation, championed by Senator Phil Gramm (whose wife Wendy Gramm previously chaired the CFTC and later joined Enron's board), removes regulatory oversight of over-the-counter (OTC) derivatives including credit default swaps (CDS). The CFMA exempts most swaps from oversight by the CFTC and SEC, allowing these instruments to grow explosively without position limits, margin requirements, or transparency mandates. The Act creates the notorious "Enron loophole" by exempting energy trading on electronic platforms from CFTC oversight, which Enron exploits extensively before its 2001 collapse causing $74 billion in shareholder losses. The deregulation enables AIG's Financial Products division to sell hundreds of billions in credit default swaps without capital reserves, contributing directly to AIG's 2008 collapse requiring $182 billion in taxpayer bailouts. The CFMA shields the shadow banking system's explosive growth in unregulated derivatives, which reaches $600 trillion in notional value by 2008. Critics identify the CFMA's deregulation of OTC derivatives as facilitating the buildup of systemic risks that contribute centrally to the 2008 financial crisis and Great Recession. Treasury Secretary Larry Summers and Fed Chairman Alan Greenspan strongly support the deregulation, overriding warnings from CFTC Chair Brooksley Born who attempted to regulate derivatives in 1998 but was blocked. The Act demonstrates how industry capture of financial regulation, revolving door corruption (Wendy Gramm), and ideological commitment to deregulation can create catastrophic systemic risks, transferring trillions in eventual losses to taxpayers while financial executives profit.
