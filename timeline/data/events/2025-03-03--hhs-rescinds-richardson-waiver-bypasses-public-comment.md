The Department of Health and Human Services rescinded the "Richardson Waiver," a 54-year-old policy that required public notice and comment before HHS could issue regulations affecting grants, benefits, and contracts. The elimination allows the agency to bypass transparency requirements and accelerate deregulation without public input.

The Richardson Waiver, established in 1971 under HHS Secretary Elliot Richardson, had voluntarily extended public participation beyond what the Administrative Procedure Act strictly required. It ensured that stakeholders—including patients, healthcare providers, and advocacy groups—could weigh in on rules affecting them.

By rescinding the waiver, HHS can now issue binding guidance and rules affecting billions in federal spending and millions of beneficiaries without notice or opportunity for comment. Critics warned the change would allow industry-favorable policies to be implemented before affected parties can organize opposition.

The timing—during the administration's first 100 days—signaled intent to move quickly on deregulation while minimizing public resistance. Healthcare advocates noted that major changes to Medicare, Medicaid, and other programs could now be implemented without the transparency that had characterized HHS rulemaking for over half a century.
