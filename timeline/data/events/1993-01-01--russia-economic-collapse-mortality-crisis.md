Russia experienced an unprecedented peacetime demographic catastrophe as shock therapy economic reforms triggered a mortality crisis that killed an estimated 1.6 to 5 million Russians through excess deaths between 1990 and 2001. Male life expectancy plummeted from 64 years in 1990 to 57 years by 1994—a drop of more than six years in just four years, representing the lowest life expectancy in the industrial world. By 1998, overall life expectancy had decreased from 70 to 65 years. Death rates increased from 1% in the 1980s to over 1.5% in 1994, equivalent to over 700,000 additional deaths annually. The massive collapse in output and living standards without war, epidemic, or natural disaster was unprecedented in modern history. Russian GDP contracted by 40-50% between 1991 and 1998 (some estimates say 50% between 1992-1998), which was significantly worse than the 30% decline faced by Western countries during the Great Depression. Industrial production declined by 56% in the same period. By 1993, the budget deficit financed by money printing was one-fifth of GDP, causing hyperinflation that decimated savings and wages. Birth rates collapsed while crime and human trafficking became widespread. Evidence showed that alcohol consumption spiked as Russians struggled with economic devastation, social dislocation, and the collapse of Soviet-era social services and safety nets. The decline in life expectancy could not be attributed simply to impoverishment but to the impact of social and economic transition exacerbated by lack of social cohesion. Coupled with its disproportionate impact on working-age males, the mortality crisis had significant negative economic impacts. The human cost of shock therapy reforms vastly exceeded the death tolls from typical economic recessions, representing what some scholars called 'economic genocide' against the Russian population.
