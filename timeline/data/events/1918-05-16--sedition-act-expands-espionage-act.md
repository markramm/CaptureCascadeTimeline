Congress passed the Sedition Act, extending the Espionage Act of 1917 to cover a broader range of offenses, notably speech and expression that cast the government or war effort in a negative light or interfered with sale of government bonds. The act forbade use of "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the United States government, its flag, or its armed forces or that caused others to view the American government or its institutions with contempt. Those convicted generally received sentences of imprisonment for five to 20 years. The act also allowed the Postmaster General to refuse to deliver mail meeting those standards for punishable speech or opinion. The final vote was 48 to 26 in the Senate and 293 to 1 in the House, with the sole dissenting vote cast by Socialist Meyer London of New York. More than 2,000 cases were filed under the Espionage Act of 1917 and Sedition Act of 1918. Of these, more than 1,000 ended in convictions. Targets of prosecution were typically individuals who opposed the war effort, including pacifists, anarchists, and socialists. Violations could lead to as much as 20 years in prison and a $10,000 fine. Notable cases included Mollie Steimer, convicted under the Espionage Act as amended by the Sedition Act. In May 1918, sedition charges were laid against Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society president Joseph Rutherford and seven other directors. On June 21, seven directors including Rutherford were sentenced to the maximum 20 years' imprisonment for each of four charges, to be served concurrently. In 1919, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the Espionage Act in Schenck v. United States, ruling that the First Amendment did not protect pacifists who circulated antidraft literature from arrest. Congress repealed the Sedition Act amendments in March 1921, though many portions of the Espionage Act of 1917 remain law today.
