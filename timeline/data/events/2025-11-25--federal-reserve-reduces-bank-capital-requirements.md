The Federal Reserve, FDIC, and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency jointly issued final rules reducing Enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio (eSLR) requirements for the nation's largest banks, weakening capital safeguards implemented after the 2008 financial crisis.

The rule reduces the amount of capital systemically important banks must hold against their assets, freeing up billions for lending, trading, or shareholder returns. The changes particularly benefit the eight largest US banks designated as Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs), including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo.

Banking industry lobbyists had advocated for the changes for years, arguing that post-crisis requirements were too stringent and constrained lending. Consumer advocates and former regulators warned that reducing capital buffers during economic uncertainty increases systemic risk and the likelihood of future taxpayer bailouts.

The timing—during the Thanksgiving holiday week when public attention was minimal—drew criticism from transparency advocates. The rule represents the most significant rollback of post-2008 financial safeguards to date, moving banking regulation closer to the pre-crisis framework that enabled the financial collapse.
