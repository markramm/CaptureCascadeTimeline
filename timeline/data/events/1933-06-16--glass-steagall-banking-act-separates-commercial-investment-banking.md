President Franklin D. Roosevelt signs the Banking Act of 1933 (Pub. L. 73-66, 48 Stat. 162) into law on June 16, 1933, enacting sweeping banking reforms including the separation of commercial banking from investment banking and establishing the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). Sponsored by Senator Carter Glass (D-VA) and Representative Henry B. Steagall (D-AL), the Act's Glass-Steagall provisions in sections 16, 20, 21, and 32 prohibit commercial banks that take deposits and make loans from underwriting or dealing in securities, while investment banks that underwrite and deal in securities cannot have close connections to commercial banks through overlapping directorships or common ownership. Banks are given one year to decide whether to operate as commercial or investment banks, with only 10 percent of commercial bank income permitted from securities. The law creates fundamental Depression-era safeguards against conflicts of interest and excessive risk-taking with depositors' federally-insured money, preventing banks from gambling with customer deposits in speculative securities markets. The Act remains largely intact for over 50 years, successfully preventing major banking crises during this period. However, provisions are gradually eroded beginning in the 1980s through regulatory interpretations and Federal Reserve exemptions, culminating in the 1999 Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act that repeals the affiliation restrictions after massive banking industry lobbying campaigns including over $85 million in 1997-1998 campaign contributions. The repeal directly enables the bank consolidation wave and risky practices that cause the 2008 financial crisis, with Nobel laureate Joseph Stiglitz and others arguing the repeal was an important crisis contributor, demonstrating how systematic corporate lobbying and regulatory capture can dismantle fundamental economic protections over decades, creating the conditions for catastrophic financial collapse.
