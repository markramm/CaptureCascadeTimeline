Colorado National Guard troops and private guards employed by John D. Rockefeller Jr.'s Colorado Fuel & Iron Company attacked a tent colony of 1,200 striking coal miners and families in Ludlow, Colorado, killing approximately 21 people in what historian Howard Zinn called "the culminating act of perhaps the most violent struggle between corporate power and laboring men in American history." After a day-long battle, Guard troops "soaked the tents in kerosene and set them on fire." In one cellar, "11 children and 2 women were found burned and suffocated." Strikers had been evicted from company towns after demanding better conditions in mines where the death rate was 7.06 per 1,000 employees, more than double the national rate of 3.15. Rockefeller Jr., who managed CF&I from his New York offices at 26 Broadway, was "widely blamed for having orchestrated the massacre" after testifying before Congress about the strike. The massacre sparked ten days of armed rebellion across 225 miles of Colorado before President Wilson sent federal troops. The strike collapsed on December 10, 1914 without union recognition, but the site became a National Historic Landmark in 2009, memorializing "the deadliest strike in the history of the United States."
