New York City extends McKinsey's contract for Rikers Island work to a total of $27.5 million despite growing evidence that violence is not decreasing and oversight bodies are questioning what the city is getting for its money. The contract had grown from an initial $1.8 million in 2014 to $6 million by spring 2015, then to $13.5 million by November 2015, and now reaches its final total of $27.5 million.

By fall 2016, oversight bodies including the city comptroller are starting to question the value of McKinsey's work. Despite the firm's reported success with the Restart units, overall violence at Rikers continues to climb. The expensive data analytics products that McKinsey persuaded the Department of Correction to purchase are not working very well, and many are never actually used by jail staff.

A senior McKinsey partner leading the Rikers work asks top corrections officials and consulting team members to restrict their communications to Wickr, an encrypted messaging app that automatically deletes messages after a few hours or days. This tactic insulates some of McKinsey's work from government oversight and public records requests, hiding the firm's failures from accountability.

The contract extension represents a classic pattern of consulting firm lock-in: initial low-cost engagement grows exponentially as the client becomes dependent on consultants who promise solutions are just one more phase away. The city spends over 15 times the initial contract value with precious little to show for it except failed algorithms and unusable data products.
