In Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States (221 U.S. 1), Chief Justice Edward White's majority opinion introduced the "rule of reason" doctrine, fundamentally transforming American antitrust law from prohibiting any restraint of trade to only prohibiting "unreasonable" restraints. White argued that the Sherman Act's vague language "necessarily called for the exercise of judgement" and established a balancing test allowing monopolistic behavior unless it led to higher prices, reduced output, or reduced quality. Justice John Marshall Harlan issued a scathing partial dissent, concurring with the Standard Oil breakup but warning that the rule of reason represented "judicial legislation"—usurpation of Congress's legislative function. Harlan argued that the 1897 Trans-Missouri Freight case had established that Congress "in unequivocal words declared that 'every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of commerce among the several states,' shall be illegal" with no distinction between reasonable and unreasonable restraints. Progressives immediately decried the decision. William Jennings Bryan declared "The Trusts Have Won." In 1913, the Court used the rule to uphold a combination controlling over 80% of shoemaking as merely introducing "greater efficiency." The doctrine's unpredictability led Congress to pass the 1914 Clayton Act and create the FTC to combat unfair business practices—but the rule of reason remained the judicial standard, enabling a century of corporate consolidation. As Harlan predicted, the ambiguous standard left companies and the public confused about what was legal, fundamentally weakening antitrust enforcement at the moment of its greatest apparent victory.
