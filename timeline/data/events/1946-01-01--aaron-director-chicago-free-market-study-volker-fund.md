Aaron Director joins the University of Chicago Law School faculty to lead the Free Market Study (1946-1953), also called the "Hayek Project" by Henry Simons and Dean Wilber Katz, initiating the first systematic corporate-funded program to reshape legal scholarship toward free-market ideology. Friedrich Hayek convinces Missouri businessman Harold Luhnow, head of the libertarian William Volker Fund, to provide founding funding, with Volker paying Director's university salary for five years and later seven years total, a rare occurrence demonstrating corporate influence over academic appointments. The Free Market Study examines legal foundations of capitalism and seeks to create reconstituted liberalism to counter collectivism, giving birth to Chicago "neoliberal" ideas. From 1946 throughout the 1950s, corporations crucially support Chicago law and economics through funding and advice, with General Motors, Sunkist, Standard Oil of Indiana, Kellogg, the Foundation for Economic Education, Rockefeller Building, and the Chamber of Commerce requesting copies of lectures and expressing gratitude for "conservative economics." This close relationship between Chicago law and economics and the corporate world establishes the template for academic capture 25 years before the Powell Memo, demonstrating that corporate strategy to reshape institutions through universities predated Powell's 1971 blueprint. Director's work fundamentally reshapes antitrust law to favor corporations, arguing that private monopolies only emerge from government action and challenging the status quo antitrust positions that undermined corporate power.
