# Smith-Connally Act Restricts Union Rights and Bans Political Contributions

Congress passes the Smith-Connally Act on June 25, 1943, overriding President Franklin D. Roosevelt's veto. The legislation responds to 1,200 recorded strikes from December 1941 through late summer 1942, most notably 400,000 coal miners striking for a $2-a-day wage increase to match wartime inflation.

## Background and Trigger

In May 1943, United Mine Workers president John L. Lewis led workers in defying the federal government by striking. Congress rushed to pass the War Labor Disputes Act in response. Roosevelt vetoed the bill partly because of his ties with labor organizations and a clause forbidding union contributions to political campaigns. For the first time during the war, Congress overrode Roosevelt's veto.

## Key Provisions

The act gave the president power to:
- Seize and operate privately owned industrial war plants when strikes interfered with production
- Require unions to give 30 days notice of intent to strike
- Mandate a cooling-off period before strikes
- Require majority support from rank-and-file membership for strikes

Any strikes in designated war plants were prohibited. War-industry unions failing to give 30 days notice were held financially liable for all damages. Most significantly, the act prohibited unions from making contributions in federal elections.

## Implementation

Despite his initial hesitancy, Roosevelt's administration relied upon the act's powers. High-profile uses included the seizure of Montgomery Ward stores and properties in April 1944, and federalizing the railroads between December 27, 1943 and January 18, 1944 when unions representing Locomotive Firemen, Railway Conductors, and Switchmen refused to withdraw strike orders.

## Legacy and Impact

The act expired six months after the war's end, as designed, but had lasting impacts. It helped expand the idea of executive power in times of emergency and served as a precursor to the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, which permanently restricted labor union power.

The ban on union political contributions established precedents for restricting labor's political influence while corporate political spending remained largely unrestricted. Some historians argue that public opinion polls supporting the act were skewed by anti-labor bias in news media and loaded questions.
