Eighty-six Luzerne County sheriff's deputies and coal company police, most armed with rifles and pistols, opened fire on 300-400 unarmed striking immigrant anthracite miners marching to support United Mine Workers organizing at Calvin Pardee's Lattimer mine near Hazleton, Pennsylvania. At 3:45 PM, deputies fired continuously "for as long as two minutes," shooting fleeing miners in the back. Nineteen men died (mostly Polish, Slovak, Lithuanian and German immigrants) with 32 wounded. The massacre was "the deadliest attack against workers in the country's history," exceeding casualties from Ludlow, Haymarket, or Homestead. Over 2,500 Pennsylvania National Guard troops were deployed that evening. Miners lived in company towns earning about $375 annually, working "in complete darkness from daybreak to dusk six days a week" with all expenses charged against their pay, keeping families in perpetual debt. In February 1898, Sheriff Martin and all deputies were acquitted despite national outrage. The Pennsylvania legislature refused to restrict the Coal and Iron Police Act of 1868 that enabled private corporate police forces. The massacre galvanized the UMW, adding over 10,000 new members and enabling the 1900 strike victory.
