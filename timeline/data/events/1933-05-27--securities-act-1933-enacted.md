President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Securities Act of 1933 into law, the first major federal legislation regulating the securities industry and a direct response to findings from the Pecora Commission's ongoing investigation. The Act established two fundamental principles: companies offering securities for public sale must provide complete and accurate information through registration statements and prospectuses, and those selling securities must not engage in deceit, misrepresentation, or fraud. The law set criminal penalties for filing false information about stock offerings, making corporate executives personally liable for the accuracy of financial disclosures. This "truth in securities" law aimed to restore investor confidence shattered by the 1929 crash and subsequent revelations of widespread fraud. The Securities Act was the first of three landmark laws directly prompted by Ferdinand Pecora's exposure of systematic corruption in banking and securities markets. Unlike the weak enforcement that would characterize financial regulation after the 2008 crisis, the 1933 Act imposed real accountability with criminal penalties for fraud. The legislation represented a fundamental shift in federal-state regulation of securities, establishing that investor protection required federal intervention. It laid the groundwork for the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which would create the Securities and Exchange Commission to enforce these new standards.
