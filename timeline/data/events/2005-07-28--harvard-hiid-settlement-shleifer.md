Harvard University agreed to pay $26.5 million to settle a federal lawsuit over the Harvard Institute for International Development (HIID) Russia scandal, while economist Andrei Shleifer agreed to pay $2 million and Jonathan Hay $1.5 million, bringing the total settlement to at least $31 million. A U.S. District Court judge had found that Shleifer and Hay 'conspired to defraud' the U.S. government while serving as lead advisors on the USAID-funded $40.4 million project to privatize Russia's economy in the 1990s. Judge Edward Woodlock found that Harvard violated the USAID contract by certifying that the Russia program was fulfilling contractual requirements even while the two men were making prohibited investments in Russian securities and companies. The court cleared Harvard of knowingly defrauding the government but found it liable for breach of contract. Shleifer, Harvard's star economist who faced a potential judgment exceeding $100 million, and Hay had been found liable for making personal investments in Russia while advising the program, violating strict conflict-of-interest provisions in their government contracts. The settlement came more than a year after the liability finding and eight years after USAID canceled the project in 1997. The mismanagement in Russia ended with the dissolution of the project and soon after the demise of HIID itself in 2000. Despite the fraud finding and massive settlement, Shleifer remained a faculty member in good standing at Harvard, which colleagues attributed to his close longtime friendship and collaboration with Lawrence Summers, who was Harvard's president during the settlement. Critics argued that the lenient treatment showed how elite academic institutions protect politically connected faculty even after major ethical violations and fraud. The Harvard Russia scandal left 'nightmarish memories and the stench of corruption in the minds of many Russians' and contributed to widespread Russian cynicism about American-promoted 'democracy' and 'market reforms.'
