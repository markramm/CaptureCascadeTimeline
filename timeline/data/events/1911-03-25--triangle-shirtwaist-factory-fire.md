A fire broke out on the top floors of the Triangle Shirtwaist factory in New York City, killing 146 garment workers—123 women and girls and 23 men—who died from the fire, smoke inhalation, or falling or jumping to their deaths. Most victims were recent Italian or Jewish immigrants aged 14 to 23 who worked nine hours a day on weekdays plus seven hours on Saturdays. The deaths were largely preventable: owners Max Blanck and Isaac Harris had locked the fire escape exit doors to prevent stealing, one stairway opened only inward, and firefighters' ladders couldn't reach the upper floors of the 10-story building. Only two of four elevators were operational. Trapped workers jumped to their deaths as witnesses watched in horror. The disaster sparked a national movement for safer working conditions. New York State Legislature created the Factory Investigating Commission in June, which over the following year and a half visited factories, interviewed workers, and held public hearings. The commission's findings led to passage of more than 30 health and safety laws, including factory fire codes and child labor restrictions. Frances Perkins, who witnessed the fire, later became Secretary of Labor under Roosevelt and helped create New Deal worker protections including the National Labor Relations Act. The 100th anniversary of the fire marked "a century of reforms that make up the core of OSHA's mission."
