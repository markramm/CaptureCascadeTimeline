The Department of Justice under Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon sues election officials in 21 states plus Washington D.C., demanding they surrender private voter registration data. The campaign, framed as enforcing federal election law, is undermined by an extraordinary accumulation of errors that erode the DOJ's credibility while it demands states meet exacting legal standards.

Documented mistakes include:

**Addressing failures:**
- Sent Wisconsin demand to the Secretary of State, who doesn't oversee elections (the bipartisan Wisconsin Elections Commission does)
- Addressed Colorado letter to "Chief Election Official for the Commonwealth of Colorado"—Colorado is a state, not a commonwealth
- Copy-pasted a Los Angeles County official reference into a Minnesota demand letter
- Misspelled "Louisiana" as "Louisianna" throughout correspondence
- Referred to Washington D.C. as a "state" demanding "statewide" voter rolls

**Legal citation errors:**
- Repeatedly cited non-existent "Driver's License Protection Act" (actual law is Driver's Privacy Protection Act)
- Referenced fake federal statute "53 U.S.C. § 21111" in proposed memoranda

**Procedural failures:**
- Left internal drafting comments visible in filed court documents, including uncertainty about whether evidence exists
- Misspelled attorney's name two different ways in Georgia complaint
- Illinois filing struck by judge for signature mismatches
- Missing required summonses, civil cover sheets, and signature compliance in multiple states

Federal courts previously found Dhillon's letter on Texas redistricting "challenging to unpack because it contain[ed] so many factual, legal, and typographical errors."

The Civil Rights Division has experienced a 75% attorney exodus under Dhillon's leadership. "Courts expect Justice Department lawyers to act honestly, in good faith, and to follow proper procedures," notes Stacey Young, executive director of Justice Connection, a DOJ alumni network. The pattern destroys the traditional "presumption of regularity" courts extend to DOJ filings.
