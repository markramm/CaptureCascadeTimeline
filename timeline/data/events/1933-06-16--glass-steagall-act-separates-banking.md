President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Glass-Steagall Banking Act into law just two days after the Senate passed it without a single dissenting vote—a unanimous passage prompted by the shocking revelations from J.P. Morgan's Pecora Commission testimony about tax evasion and the "preferred list." Sponsored by Senator Carter Glass (D-VA), a former Treasury Secretary, and Representative Henry Steagall (D-AL), chairman of the House Banking and Currency Committee, the law fundamentally restructured American banking by separating commercial banking from investment banking and creating the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) to guarantee individual bank deposits. Banks were given one year to decide whether they would be commercial banks or investment banks—they could not be both. The Act directly addressed "abuses arising out of the interrelationship of commercial and investment banking" identified by Ferdinand Pecora's investigation, including conflicts of interest such as underwriting unsound securities to pay off bad bank loans and using depositor funds for speculative investments. Glass-Steagall represented the most significant banking reform in American history, preventing the conflicts of interest that had precipitated the Great Depression. The law remained in effect for 66 years until its systematic dismantling through the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999, which allowed the re-merging of commercial and investment banking—deregulation that directly enabled the 2008 financial crisis.
