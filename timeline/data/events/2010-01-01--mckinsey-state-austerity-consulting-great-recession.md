Following the Great Recession, McKinsey significantly expands its consulting work with state and local governments facing unprecedented budget shortfalls. At the peak of the recession, U.S. state tax revenues fell 17% below their level one year earlier, and personal income taxes were 27% lower. States accumulated $500 billion in budget shortfalls from 2009 to 2012 - more than double the amount in the previous recession.

McKinsey advises state governments on 'efficiency' measures and 'operational transformations' as they implement devastating austerity policies. Some states cut elementary and secondary education funding by as much as 40%. Between 2008 and 2013, state government workforces are reduced by about 6%. Twenty-eight states drain their rainy-day funds to a point where they have less than a week's worth of operating costs; 17 use them entirely.

McKinsey's consulting work during this period promotes the idea that states can save 3 to 10% in operating expenses while maintaining service quality through operational transformation, rather than addressing the fundamental revenue crisis through progressive taxation. The firm's recommendations often prioritize cost-cutting metrics over the actual impacts on vulnerable populations who depend on public services.

This represents a pattern where elite consulting firms profit from public sector crises by advising austerity measures that gut services while avoiding questions about revenue generation or progressive taxation. McKinsey's work helps legitimize the narrative that government inefficiency, rather than political choices about taxation and spending priorities, is the root cause of budget shortfalls. The firm earns millions advising on cuts to education, healthcare, and social services while the economic crisis disproportionately harms working-class and poor communities.
