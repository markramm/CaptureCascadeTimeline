President Woodrow Wilson signs the Clayton Antitrust Act, strengthening the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 by explicitly prohibiting specific anticompetitive practices that had exploited loopholes in earlier legislation. The Act bans price discrimination between customers buying identical goods (Section 2), prohibits exclusive dealing contracts and tying arrangements that reduce competition (Section 3), restricts mergers and acquisitions that "may substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly" (Section 7), and forbids interlocking directorates where one person serves on boards of competing corporations (Section 8). Critically, Section 6 exempts labor unions and agricultural organizations from antitrust law, declaring "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce" and protecting collective bargaining, strikes, picketing, and boycotts. The Act authorizes private parties to sue for triple damages and introduces personal liability for corporate officers. Enforcement authority is shared between the newly created Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice. However, the law's effectiveness would be systematically weakened during the 1920s through pro-business court interpretations, particularly U.S. Steel Corp. (1920) ruling that mere size alone does not constitute illegal monopoly, and reduced enforcement under Republican administrations that promoted "associational" business cooperation rather than aggressive antitrust action.
