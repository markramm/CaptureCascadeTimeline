On July 9, 2021, President Biden signed Executive Order 14036, titled "Promoting Competition in the American Economy," establishing a "whole-of-government effort to promote competition" through 72 separate initiatives directed at over a dozen federal agencies—the most comprehensive attack on corporate consolidation since Franklin D. Roosevelt's antitrust supercharge in the late 1930s. The Washington Post called the order "striking in its scope and ambition," representing a fundamental rejection of four decades of permissive merger policy that had enabled unprecedented market concentration across every sector of the American economy.

The executive order explicitly invoked the historical parallel: "In the early 1900s, Teddy Roosevelt's Administration broke up the trusts controlling the economy—Standard Oil, J.P. Morgan's railroads, and others. In the late 1930s, FDR's Administration supercharged antitrust enforcement, increasing more than eightfold the number of cases brought in just two years—enforcement actions that saved consumers billions in today's dollars and helped unleash decades of sustained, inclusive economic growth." Biden framed the order as returning to this tradition after decades of Chicago School capture that treated monopolization as economically efficient.

The 72 provisions spanned multiple industries and enforcement mechanisms. Specific initiatives included: limiting non-compete clauses that trapped workers in low-wage jobs, allowing hearing aids to be sold over-the-counter, reviving net neutrality protections, scrutinizing hospital and pharmaceutical mergers, challenging agricultural monopolies, and directing the FTC and DOJ to more rigorously enforce existing antitrust laws. The order directed agencies to use their full authority under Section 5 of the FTC Act and other statutes to challenge anticompetitive practices that might not violate Sherman or Clayton Acts but nevertheless harmed workers, consumers, and innovation.

The first meeting of the White House Competition Council convened on September 10, 2021, in the Roosevelt Room, led by National Economic Council Director Brian Deese. The symbolism was deliberate: invoking Roosevelt's trust-busting legacy while creating institutional infrastructure to coordinate enforcement across fragmented regulatory agencies. The order represented not just policy guidance but a philosophical revolution, rejecting the premise that corporate consolidation is inherently efficient and recognizing that monopoly power harms democratic governance, worker power, innovation, and economic dynamism.

However, the executive order immediately faced fierce corporate resistance. Business groups challenged the legal authority behind specific provisions, lobbied Congress to defund enforcement, and launched litigation to block implementation. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and industry-specific trade associations mounted coordinated campaigns portraying the order as regulatory overreach and government interference with free markets—the same arguments used to dismantle antitrust enforcement in the 1970s and 80s. The tension between Biden's ambitious competition agenda and entrenched corporate power would define the following years, with mixed results: some merger challenges succeeded while others failed, and Trump's 2025 return to power would systematically dismantle the enforcement infrastructure the order sought to build.
