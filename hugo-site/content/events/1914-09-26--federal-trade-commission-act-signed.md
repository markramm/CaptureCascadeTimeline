President Woodrow Wilson signs the Federal Trade Commission Act into law, creating the Federal Trade Commission as an independent regulatory agency to prevent unfair methods of competition and deceptive business practices. The Senate passed the bill 43-5 on September 8, and the House followed on September 10 without a recorded vote. The five-member commission, which formally opens on March 16, 1915, receives authority to investigate price-fixing agreements, prohibit mergers and price discrimination that threaten competition, investigate deceptive advertising, and regulate packaging and labeling to prevent consumer deception. The Act represents a significant expansion of federal regulatory capacity beyond the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, providing business and legal experts with power to force companies into "consent decrees" as an alternative to lengthy court battles. The FTC complements the Clayton Antitrust Act, also passed in 1914, forming the core of U.S. antitrust legislation. However, the agency's effectiveness would be systematically undermined during the pro-business 1920s under Republican administrations, demonstrating the vulnerability of Progressive Era regulatory institutions to political capture and weakened enforcement.
