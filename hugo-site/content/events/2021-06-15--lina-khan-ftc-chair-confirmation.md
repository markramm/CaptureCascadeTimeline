On June 15, 2021, the U.S. Senate confirmed 32-year-old Lina Khan as Federal Trade Commission Commissioner by a bipartisan vote of 69-28, and within hours President Biden appointed her as FTC Chair, making her the youngest person ever to lead the agency. Khan's rapid ascent represented a dramatic shift in antitrust philosophy, installing a progressive reformer who gained prominence through her 2017 Yale Law Journal article "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox," which fundamentally challenged four decades of market concentration enabled by the Chicago School's consumer welfare standard.

Khan's groundbreaking scholarship argued that current standards for assessing anticompetitive behavior—in place since the 1970s and 80s and emphasizing short-term effects on consumer prices—were inadequate to understanding the nature of Amazon's structural power within the digital economy. Her work received the 2018 Antitrust Writing Award for Best Academic Unilateral Conduct Article and gained the attention of prominent legislators across the political spectrum. Former FTC Chairman William Kovacic noted: "She brings to the job what I would call the boldest vision for the agency in its history."

Khan's bipartisan Senate confirmation was attributed to her "influential anti-Amazon views" being widely reflected in Congress, demonstrating rare agreement across party lines about the dangers of Big Tech monopolization. However, corporate opposition materialized immediately. Within months of her appointment, both Amazon and Meta Platforms filed petitions with the FTC seeking her recusal from investigations of their companies, arguing that her past criticism left her unable to be impartial—an unprecedented corporate attack on a regulator's credentials and a preview of the systematic resistance Khan would face.

The appointment signaled Biden's commitment to aggressive antitrust enforcement after decades of regulatory capture. Khan joined other progressive enforcers including Jonathan Kanter at DOJ Antitrust and Timothy Wu at the National Economic Council, creating the most reform-minded competition policy team since the New Deal. Her installation represented not just a personnel change but a philosophical revolution: rejecting the premise that corporate consolidation benefits consumers through efficiency, and instead examining how monopoly power harms workers, suppliers, innovation, and democratic governance itself.

The tension was immediate and fundamental. Khan advocated using FTC Section 5 authority to challenge "unfair methods of competition" beyond what Sherman or Clayton Acts could reach, while corporate interests and their captured regulators insisted on narrow price-focused metrics that had enabled four decades of unprecedented consolidation. Khan would serve as FTC Chairwoman from 2021 to 2025, until being replaced by Trump appointee Andrew Ferguson on January 20, 2025, ending the most consequential attempted antitrust revival in generations.
