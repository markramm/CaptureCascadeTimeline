Warren G. Harding's inauguration marks the beginning of systematic pro-business transformation of Progressive Era regulatory agencies, particularly the Federal Trade Commission. Harding appoints Herbert Hoover as Secretary of Commerce, transforming what had been a minor department focused on lighthouses and fisheries into a powerful organization promoting business cooperation and "associationalism." Under Hoover's direction, the Commerce Department fosters voluntary trade associations where competitors exchange information on prices, markets, and products, positioning industry self-regulation as a "middle way between competition and monopoly." The FTC shifts from aggressive enforcement to business-friendly cooperation, with antitrust suits declining sharply. By 1926, the FTC creates a Division of Trade Practice Conference that forestalls legal action against alleged antitrust violators by organizing voluntary industry-by-industry agreements about "fair trade practices." This voluntarism reflects Republican faith in business capacity for self-regulation without government intervention, effectively making the FTC an ally of "respectable" business rather than a corporate watchdog. The administration's policies represent near-complete reversal to Gilded Age laissez-faire politics, systematically weakening the regulatory framework established during the Progressive Era through political capture of enforcement agencies, demonstrating how electoral transitions enable corporate interests to neutralize reform institutions without repealing underlying legislation.
