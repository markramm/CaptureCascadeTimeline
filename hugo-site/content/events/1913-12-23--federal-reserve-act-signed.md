President Woodrow Wilson signs the Federal Reserve Act at 6:00 PM, creating the Federal Reserve System as the central banking authority of the United States. The Senate adopted the conference report 43-25 earlier that day, with every Democrat voting for the measure and all but four Republicans opposed. The legislation's technical structure closely resembles the plan drafted three years earlier at a secret November 1910 meeting on Jekyll Island, Georgia, where Senator Nelson Aldrich and banking executives representing J.P. Morgan, Rockefeller, and Kuhn Loeb & Co. designed the framework during ten days of confidential deliberation. Despite public opposition to banker control and accusations that Aldrich was biased due to his daughter's marriage to John D. Rockefeller Jr., the final bill maintains the Jekyll Island plan's core architecture while making political concessions to progressive and populist Democrats. Some congressmen, including Representatives Murdock and Lindbergh, claim New York bankers feigned opposition to induce passage through "sham battle." The Act creates a new monetary system designed to prevent financial panics but also establishes institutional mechanisms through which private banking interests exercise significant influence over national credit policy, demonstrating how corporate elites shape regulatory frameworks ostensibly designed to constrain them.
