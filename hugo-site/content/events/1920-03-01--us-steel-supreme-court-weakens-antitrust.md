The U.S. Supreme Court rules 4-3 in United States v. United States Steel Corporation that corporate size alone does not violate antitrust law, effectively ending aggressive antitrust enforcement for decades. Justice Joseph McKenna writes for the majority that "the law does not make mere size an offense, or the existence of unexerted power an offense" and that corporations cannot be found guilty based solely on market share or potential power without proof of actual anticompetitive conduct. The decision shields U.S. Steel, which controlled approximately 80 percent of American steel production after consolidating 180 formerly independent companies, from dissolution under the Sherman Act. This "mere size" doctrine reverses the thrust of Progressive Era antitrust enforcement by establishing that the rule of reason requires demonstrating overt anticompetitive acts rather than structural market dominance. The ruling immediately triggers a new merger wave across American industries and essentially ends governmental enforcement of Sherman Act Section 2 until the Alcoa case in 1945. Economic scholars later identify this decision as ratifying "the most socially damaging of all mergers in U.S. history" by making the steel industry permanently less competitive and establishing precedent that corporations could achieve monopolistic scale without legal consequences, demonstrating how judicial interpretation systematically undermined Progressive Era regulatory frameworks.
