The Pujo Committee, led by Louisiana Representative Arsene Pujo and chief counsel Samuel Untermyer, issues its final report after 18 months of investigation (May 1912 - February 1913) documenting the existence of a powerful "Money Trust" controlling U.S. financial markets. The investigation reveals that six banking houses - J.P. Morgan & Co., First National Bank of New York, National City Bank, Lee Higginson & Co., Kidder Peabody & Co., and Kuhn Loeb & Co. - exercise concentrated control over major manufacturing, transportation, mining, telecommunications, and financial corporations through 341 interlocking directorships in 112 corporations valued at over $22 billion in resources and capitalization. The report documents how Morgan and Rockefeller banking empires systematically consolidated power through stock holdings, interlocking boards, and coordinated control of credit allocation. These findings, widely publicized through Louis Brandeis's book "Other People's Money and How the Bankers Use It," generate massive public support for Progressive Era reforms including the Federal Reserve Act, Clayton Antitrust Act, and Federal Trade Commission Act. The investigation's exposure of systematic financial concentration demonstrates the institutional capture mechanisms that would be studied and replicated throughout the 20th century.
